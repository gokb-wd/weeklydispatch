## This week's opinion:
### 1. Should you become a specialist?

### The Prestige of Specialisation (Handle With Care)
Modern culture adores the specialist.
Narrow mastery is paraded as seriousness, discipline, and adulthood.
*Pick a lane early, stay in it forever, and never look sideways*.
The generalist, meanwhile, is treated like someone who couldn’t sit still long enough to become respectable.
This view survives mainly because it sounds tidy.
Reality isn't tidy.
Reality doesn't reward obedience to intellectual zoning laws.
It rewards people who can see the whole mess and still make sense of it.

If that description makes you uncomfortable, good. That discomfort is doing useful work.

### Where Problems Actually Live
Real problems do not belong to departments.
They sprawl. They leak. They borrow tools from wherever they can find them.
Law bleeds into economics. Economics bleeds into psychology. Psychology bleeds into incentives. Incentives quietly bleed into everything.
Generalists live at these intersections.
They see the shape of the problem before it finishes introducing itself. Specialists often arrive later, highly prepared to solve a much cleaner version of a problem that no longer exists.
Depth without context is impressive, but it is also a reliable way to be precisely wrong.

### Skills That Travel With You
A specialist often spends years turning effort into expertise that only works in one climate.
Change the weather and the skill starts to wheeze.
A generalist converts experience into mental models that survive relocation.
Pattern recognition. First-principles thinking. Negotiation. Synthesis. Judgment.
These skills don’t belong to any one field, which is exactly why they keep paying rent no matter where they're used.
They improve the more domains they are exposed to. They compound quietly. They age well. They don't panic when a new tool (such as Gen AI) shows up and steals everyone’s lunch.

### Volatility Separates the Flexible From the Fragile
History is repetitive - in a boring way.
Industries reshuffle. Technologies wipe out entire skill stacks. Prestige migrates without apology.
Stability is always temporary; it just likes to pretend otherwise.
Specialists thrive when the ground stays still. Generalists survive when it moves.
When conditions change, generalists reconfigure. They don't argue with reality or cling to sunk costs like they're sentimental heirlooms.
They pick up what still works and keep moving.

### Why Direction Beats Volume
Specialists are often rewarded for output. More cases closed. More code shipped. More procedures completed. Motion everywhere, meaning optional.
Generalists are rewarded for leverage. Choosing the right problem. Framing it properly. Coordinating the right people. Killing bad ideas early. Stopping waste before it acquires a budget and a personality.
Leverage comes from judgment. Judgment on the other hand doesn't get better just from doing the same thing multiple times.

### Incentives Run the World, Quietly
Techniques are fragile. They date quickly.
Incentives endure. They outlive tools, institutions, and most reforms.
Generalists pay attention to incentives across systems. They understand why people behave the way they do, even when the official story says otherwise. This makes prediction possible and negotiation effective.
Many disasters blamed on technical failure are simply incentive failures wearing a lab coat.

### Thinking Past the First Move
Narrow expertise optimises locally. Broad understanding evaluates consequences. Ethics, second-order effects, downstream incentives, unintended fallout—all of these transcend disciplinary borders.
Power applied without this awareness produces elegant failures. They look competent right up until they collapse.
Generalists are harder to impress and harder to surprise, which is a useful trait in systems that punish naïveté.

### Autonomy Is Built, Not Granted
Careers tied to a single niche depend on demand staying polite forever.
Generalists build optionality. They can pivot, combine roles, consult, design work, and exit gracefully. Autonomy follows from being difficult to box in. Replaceability is the tax paid for narrow perfection.

### Depth Without Confinement
This is not a defence of laziness. Competence matters. Skill matters. Depth matters. Anyone using “generalist” as a cover for intellectual sloppiness should be escorted out gently but firmly.
No, this is an argument against confinement. Strong generalists choose depth. They integrate like it's going out of fashion. They know enough to ask uncomfortable questions, detect fake certainty, and call in true specialists anytime precision is required. They understand that humility is different from ignorance.

### How Progress Actually Happens
Civilisations advance by synthesis. Law bends to economics. Economics bends to psychology. Psychology bends to biology. Biology bends to environment. Environment bends to technology. Anyone insisting these layers stay separate is either confused or selling something.
Progress belongs to those who can hold multiple layers at once without flattening them into slogans.

### Refusing to Outsource Understanding
The future won't reward fence-guarding. It will reward boundary-crossing.
The generalist mindset is not a failure to specialise.
It is a refusal to outsource understanding to titles, silos, or fashionable credentials.
Choose depth when it matters. Choose breadth so you know when it matters.

To finish, here's a quote by Robert Heinlein:
> "A human being should be able to change a diaper, plan an invasion, butcher a hog, conn a ship, design a building, write a sonnet, balance accounts, build a wall, set a bone, comfort the dying, take orders, give orders, cooperate, act alone, solve equations, analyze a new problem, pitch manure, program a computer, fight efficiently, die gallantly.  Specialization is for insects."
